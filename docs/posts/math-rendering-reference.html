Matrices and vectors

A product of two matrices, and a column vector:

A=(1234),v→=(123) A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}

Written elementwise:

(AB)ij=∑k=1naikbkj (AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} b_{kj}

Determinants

det⁡A=∣abcd∣=ad−bc \det A = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = ad - bc

The Vandermonde determinant — deliberately wide, to exercise horizontal scrolling on narrow screens:

(1x1⋯x1n−11x2⋯x2n−1⋮⋮⋱⋮1xn⋯xnn−1)=∏1≤i<j≤n(xj−xi) \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_1 & \cdots & x_1^{n-1} \\ 1 & x_2 & \cdots & x_2^{n-1} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_n & \cdots & x_n^{n-1} \end{pmatrix} = \prod_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} (x_j - x_i)

Inline math

The complexity 𝒪(nlog⁡n)\mathcal{O}(n \log n) beats 𝒪(n2)\mathcal{O}(n^2). The Euclidean norm of v→\vec{v} is written ∥v→∥2\|\vec{v}\|_2, and the positive reals are ℝ>0\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

Note how inline math sits on the baseline without disturbing the leading of the surrounding paragraph, even when the expression is tall like a+bc+d\frac{a+b}{c+d}.
